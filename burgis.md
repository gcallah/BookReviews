Burgis


Luke Burgis has written an interesting book exploring the tension between belonging and differentiation. This theme was beautifully symbolized by Albert Camus in his short story "The Artist at Work," from his book *Exile and the Kingdom*. The artist frequently paints in his garret while a group of his friends socialize below him, torn between his desire to be with his friends and to create his art. One day, when he fails to come down from the garret as expected, his friends climb up, only to find him in his chair. Before him is a painting, the canvas bearing just a single word. But the friends cannot tell if what he painted was "solitary" or "solidary."




His technique of interspersing  stories of his father stories of his father throughout the text are, I think, an effective way to help the reader relate to the book on a personal level. 

The parable that gave the book its title is from the Gospel of Luke: A single sheep wanders far from the flock. The shepherd leaves ninety-nine sheep behind to go in search of the one lost sheep. 

Burgis presents this as a puzzle: "Who in their right mind risks ninety-nine to save one?" (10) He goes on to say:

"It took me fifteen years to see what was hiding in plain sight. In the parable of the lost sheep, the shepherd does not choose between belonging and differentiation. He wants to bring the one lost sheep back into the fold, but he knows and cares for each of the other ninety-nine individually." (12)

That is all well and good, but it does nothing to resolve the puzzle Burgis presented us with two pages earlier: it is still the case that the shepherd risked a pack of wolves descending on the rest of his flock while he was away looking for the one lost sheep. The author has offered us no explanation of why this was a sensible decision.  One cannot resolve a dilemma by wishing it away.

In any case, that puzzle leads him to the book's premise: 

"Each of us is torn between belonging and differentiation, and few ever learn to manage that tension. We might not even realize it exists. Once we become aware of the tension and learn to navigate it, we can live with far more freedom -- and far more integrity -- than ever." (11)

Without becoming aware of this tension, Burgis argues, we become highly vulnerable to social contagion and peer pressure. And, he adds, this problem has been made much worse by social media. 

His solution to this problem is to develop what he calls a "solid self," as opposed to a "pseudo-self." Well, the intention behind this distinction is good... He wants us to have a firm sense of our values, so that we cannot be lured into bad choices by the weight of opinion of those we were with at the time... I find the terminology a little bit odd. After all, Jesus told us "Whoever wants to be my disciple must deny themselves and take up their cross and follow me." That does not sound like they are making a more "solid self." And Saint Augustine told us to give up the amor sui and instead live with amor dei. The teachings of the Buddha were similar in this regard, and, as Leonard Cohen put it, the light gets in through the cracks.

Of course, Burgis does not mean that we should create a "solid self" by becoming totally self-absorbed and stoking our own ego, but that is why I doubt that was the best term for what he wished to designate.

Burgis then introduces the idea of a "value response." He posits a hierarchy of importance, where some of our actions may aim at some end because it is subjectively satisfying, others because the end is socially important, and others because it is objectively good for us. 

But whereas Burgis treats these as distinct categories, they seem to me to be matters of degree, each of them present in any action. For instance, he considers his plan to have another cup of coffee as aiming at a purely subjective good. but it seems he enjoys writing more when he has a hot drink at hand. In this sense, he was aiming something objectively good for him, namely to get his work done to to earn his sustenance. And if he hopes his writing will be read by others, the action is socially important.

Burgis attempts to differentiate what is merely subjectively satisfying by pointing out that, for these goods, "their importance evaporates when circumstances change." (33) But that confuses the transitoriness of their desirability with subjectivity. The very good he uses an example of what is objectively good for him, working out at the gym, is also not continually desirable. For instance, if he had just finished a long and taxing workout, going back into the gym and working out again would turn out to be objectively bad for him. 

in discussing social influence, Burgis holds the Nazi party up as an example of pernicious influence because "only the values that served its ends were held up." But was that really the problem with the Nazi party? Wasn't it, rather, that the ends it sought were evil? Doesn't, for instance, the Catholic Church only "hold up" values that serve its end of saving souls?

Burgis includes a little table demonstrating the transformation, across seven realms, of how they search for quick hits of dopamine have come to dominate our behavior. For instance, whereas in traditional culture, one would play a sport, I gradually became more common to watch a sport, and finally, to continually gamble on a sport, with the ability to place bets online on things like whether Ohtani will hit a home run this next at-bat.


Burgis continues by analyzing how group dynamics in the family interact with the struggle for differentiation. In doing so, he claims, "Every family system takes shape in emotional triangles. The smallest stable unit is never two people, but three." (54) Now, I don't doubt that it is usually possible to analyze family dynamics in terms of triangles. But is analysis in terms of triangles always more fundamental than binary analysis, or analysis in terms of rectangular or pentangularc relationships?

In any case,  Burgis makes the plausible case that to achieve differentiation in social situations in general, we first must achieve it in the context of our original social situation, the family in which we grew up. 




The book is often sloppy with the examples it uses. Frogs won't actually sit in a pot of heating water until they're boiled. Moths do not flutter around porch lights because they fly towards light. (They actually try to keep their back towards the light so they stay upright.) By the 1990s, contra Burgis, personal computers were already pretty widespread.  At another point, four critiques of technology are introduced, including Heidegger's... but then they don't ever seem to be mentioned again.

The unsentimental home



Burgis is often careless with the examples he uses. Frogs won't actually sit in a pot of heating water until they're boiled. Moths do not flutter around porch lights because they fly towards light. (They actually try to keep their back towards the light so they stay upright.) By the 1990s, contra Burgis, personal computers were already pretty widespread.  At another point, four critiques of technology are introduced, including Heidegger's... but then they don't ever seem to be mentioned again. So why bring them up? Diogenes masturbating in public is introduced as though it were some sort of bold act, creating a "solid self," rather than merely being creepy. Burgis writes that "before AI," the trend towards working from home was already well underway, but "AI" has existed since about 1959. (AI is a marketing term, rather than any actual analytical category of software, but the hype around it began decades ago.)

Nevertheless, this is not a bad book. It is filled with interesting insights and ideas. But I think it could have been a much better book, if only it had taken more time to find its solid self.
