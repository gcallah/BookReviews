# Pitch campaign: Bryan Caplan’s *Unbeatable*

**Prepared:** November 12, 2026  
**Book:** Bryan Caplan, *Unbeatable: The Brutally Honest Case for Free Markets* (University of Chicago Press; 352 pages; scheduled for spring 2027).

## Executive recommendation

Lead with **Reason** and **The New Republic**. They are the strongest combination of subject fit, editorial reach, and a clear route for a freelance approach. In parallel, prepare pitches for **The Los Angeles Review of Books** and **The Baffler**. The strongest paid-rate leads are LARB (published standard rates) and Current Affairs (published rates, but see its strict AI rule below); Reason and The Baffler confirm that they pay, but do not publish a current rate.

Your most valuable credential is straightforward: **you have professionally published 86 book reviews**. Put that fact near the top of every pitch, then substantiate it with three excellent, relevant clips. Do not invent a list of outlets or subject specialties: add those details once you have selected the strongest examples.

The publisher describes Caplan’s book as a broad, maximalist case for markets that acknowledges the downsides of market policies while arguing that they should be judged by their overall performance. Its table of contents explicitly takes on economists’ views of markets and government, irrationality, and the gap between what policy ought to do and what it probably will do. Those features offer a more interesting review than a simple endorsement or denunciation.  
Sources: [University of Chicago Press book page](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/U/bo287993614.html); [Caplan’s announcement of the spring 2027 publication](https://www.betonit.ai/p/acknowledgements-for-unbeatable).

## 2. Two tailored pitch angles

### A. Reason: a rigorous test of the free-market case

**Proposed headline:** *How Strong Is the Case for Free Markets?*

**Angle:** Caplan is not merely repeating the usual case for markets. The book’s distinctive promise is that it acknowledges the ugly downsides of market policies and argues that institutions should be judged by their overall performance rather than their emotional appeal. The review would test whether this method produces a genuinely persuasive case: Which objections does Caplan meet head-on? Which trade-offs does he understate? Does he make a convincing comparison between imperfect markets and imperfect government?

**Why Reason might care:** It is a book about the magazine’s central subject, but the proposed review offers scrutiny rather than ideological affirmation. Reason’s guidelines specifically ask for original analysis rather than a restatement of familiar libertarian positions.

**Suggested length:** Ask the editor; a web review in the 1,000–1,500-word range could be a reasonable starting proposal, while Reason’s online articles are often shorter.

### B. The New Republic: markets, government, and political judgment

**Proposed headline:** *The Case for Markets in an Age of Disillusionment*

**Angle:** Caplan’s book can be read as an argument about how citizens should judge institutions. People often judge markets and government by their most vivid failures, but Caplan argues for evaluating them by overall performance. The review would examine whether that comparison is fair, what it leaves out about political power and market power, and whether the book offers a persuasive answer to readers who are skeptical of market solutions.

**Why TNR might care:** The book opens onto a larger political question: what should government do, what can markets do better, and how should the public weigh imperfect alternatives? The review would be analytical rather than a book report or a partisan endorsement.

**Suggested length:** Propose a substantial Books & the Arts review essay, with the precise length left to Laura Marsh.

## 4. What to assemble before sending

1. **Choose three clips.** Prefer reviews that show range, fairness, command of ideas, and an ability to evaluate an argument—not simply three pieces on books that agree with one another.
2. **Create a one-page review résumé.** State “86 professionally published book reviews,” then list selected outlets, subjects reviewed, and links. Include dates only if useful.
3. **Prepare a short bio.** Two or three sentences. Mention relevant teaching, research, professional experience, or subject knowledge only if it directly supports this assignment.
4. **Check the publisher’s publicity contact for an advance review copy.** The University of Chicago Press book page is the authoritative source for the publication details. Ask for a digital galley or ARC and confirm when it will be available.
5. **Ask about the assignment terms.** Before beginning, confirm the fee, word count, deadline, publication date, rights, exclusivity, and whether the editor is commissioning a review or considering a completed draft.
6. **Keep a simple pitch log.** Record the outlet, editor, date sent, version of the pitch, response date, and follow-up date.

When in doubt, pitch sequentially and keep the editor informed if another outlet is considering the same piece.

## 6. Negotiation checklist

When an editor expresses interest, ask:

- What is the fee, and when is it paid?
- Is the piece commissioned, or are they considering a completed review on spec?
- What word count and deadline do they want?
- Will they provide an advance copy, and when?
- Is the assignment exclusive? For how long?
- What rights are they buying (first publication, print, digital, syndication, archival use)?
- Is there a kill fee if the review is accepted as an assignment but not published?
- May you link to the review in your portfolio after publication?

A concise rate question is entirely appropriate: **“Could you let me know the word count, deadline, and fee you have budgeted for the review?”**

## 7. Final recommendation

Start with **Reason and The New Republic**, but do not make the pitch sound like the same piece sent to two ideological camps. For Reason, emphasize the intellectual stress test of Caplan’s case. For TNR, emphasize the broader political question of how markets and government should be compared.

Your 86-review record is the strongest selling point. Put it in the first half of the email, attach three strong clips, and keep the pitch focused on a distinctive argument the editor could imagine publishing—not merely on the fact that a prominent economist has a new book.
